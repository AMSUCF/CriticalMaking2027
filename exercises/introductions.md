# Discussion One: Introductions

## Think: Provocations

Welcome to Critical Making! This course might be different from most you've taken in the past. It's fully online and asynchronous, so there are no required meetings on Zoom or in person, and you'll be making strange things with new genres and tools nearly every week.

Critical making is a form of humanities research that sits somewhere between "making," as in the maker spaces and hobbyist cultures of the last couple of decades, and "critique," as in the critical traditions of the arts and humanities. You'll also see it called research-creation. Whatever we call it, the emphasis is on the **process** (what you learn and discover as you make) rather than the final product. As Matt Ratto argues in this week's reading, the act of building is itself a way of thinking through technology and its social life. The things you make this semester will be more like prototypes or proofs-of-concept than polished masterpieces: embrace the rough edges!

This week, there's no making exercise yet. Instead, focus on the readings, which offer a set of provocations for the rest of the semester: What counts as scholarship? Who gets to be a maker? What do we learn when we build, break, and remake the tools and forms around us? If you'd like to hear from Matt Ratto directly, he offers a short [introduction to critical making](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zm0-15mtXr4) that pairs well with his chapter. And if you haven't read it before, Miriam Sweeney's ["How to Read for Grad School"](https://miriamsweeney.net/2012/06/20/readforgradschool/) is a helpful companion for a reading-heavy week.

Some works each week are marked "Explore": these are often substantial digital projects, journals, or collections provided for context and inspiration. You aren't expected to read everything in them, so browse and follow what catches your interest.

### How each week works

Starting next week, every module follows the same rhythm: **read → make → reflect.**

1. **Read.** Complete the week's readings, and, most weeks, the featured interview with a scholar or maker (available in Webcourses). These provide the framework for the making, as well as examples of what that making might look like.
2. **Make.** Work through the week's making exercise page. Each one introduces a tool or form, offers step-by-step guidance, and leaves plenty of room for you to be inventive and playful.
3. **Reflect.** Post your process documentation and reflection to the week's discussion, then respond to your peers.

This online course is not "self-paced," so I suggest setting aside time on at least three days each week: one day to carefully read and take notes (Monday?), a second to complete the making exercise (Wednesday?), and a third to post your reflection and reply to others (Friday or Saturday?).

**Deadlines are Sundays at 11:59 PM Eastern.** Each discussion stays open for no-penalty late submissions for one week after the deadline. Once a discussion closes, late work can't be accepted unless we've already arranged an extension, so please reach out early if something comes up.

Don't forget that the **Activity Verification** in Webcourses is due this Friday: it's required by the university, so please complete it as soon as possible.

### Discussion

Welcome to Critical Making! This week, please take a minute to introduce yourself and connect with your peers. In your post, share:

- **Your name** (and pronouns, if you'd like)
- **Your research interests**, and where you are in your program
- **What you hope to make** this semester, whether that's a specific tool or form you're curious about, a question you'd like to explore through making, or something you've always wanted to try
- **Optional:** a pet photo. These are always welcome!

Then, reply to **at least two classmates**: look for shared interests, ask a question, or point them toward something they might enjoy. I also encourage you to form a class Discord or other community for sharing resources outside our formal spaces.

This discussion is worth 3 points, earned by posting your introduction and replying to at least two peers. Like all of our discussions, it stays open for no-penalty late submissions for one week after the deadline.
